What we built, in plain words

GovStart Bridge · SIH26136 · a brief for the team · no technical background needed

You do not need to understand the code to talk about this project well. You need one sentence, four steps, and four clever bits. That is all this page is.

The one sentence

A government department cannot buy something new, because the rules say you must describe what you are buying — and something new is the one thing you cannot describe yet. So we let them buy the proof first, and the product afterwards.

If you remember nothing else, remember that. Everything in the project is a consequence of it.

Why this is a real problem

A district hospital has a three-hour queue. A startup has something that might fix it. The officer cannot simply buy it: the rulebook says write a specification, invite tenders, pick the cheapest that qualifies. You cannot write a specification for a thing nobody has proved works yet.

So one of two things happens. The officer does nothing, and the queue stays. Or they run a small trial, it works — and then they still have no lawful way to buy it, so it dies there.

That second one is the quiet tragedy. The pilot works and nothing happens.

What we built — four steps

1Write down what success means, and lock it The officer writes the outcome they want — “cut the wait to under an hour” — not a product. Then we lock that definition so it cannot be changed later.
2Decide who is allowed to enter Normally only big companies qualify. We measure how much damage the trial could possibly do, cap it, and then let anyone in who fits under that cap — including a company that has never worked with government.
3Run a small trial, and pay only for evidence One taluka, with real data but names removed. The startup is paid step by step, as each piece of evidence arrives. At this stage the department has bought nothing.
4Someone independent checks, then buy it properly A checker — chosen in step 1, before anyone knew who would win — confirms the results are real. If they are, the system finds a legal way to buy the product. If there is no legal way, it says so.

The four clever bits

The criteria are sealed before anyone competes
Like sealing the answer key in an envelope and signing across the flap before the exam starts. If anyone opens it and changes an answer afterwards, the signature no longer lines up — and everyone can see it.
We pay for the survey before we buy the house
Nobody buys a house from a photograph. You pay a surveyor first, then decide. We do exactly that: pay for the proof, then buy the thing.
Company size stops mattering
A bank asks for a salary slip because it cannot see whether you will repay. Offer collateral instead and the salary slip stops being the point. We cap what the trial can break — so how big the company is stops being the point.
The referee is named before the match
You do not choose the umpire after seeing the score. Our checker is named at the very start, before any result exists.

Words you will hear, and what they mean

GFRThe government’s rulebook for buying things. We do not change a single line of it.
Rule 173(i)The clause that lets you drop the “must be a big company” condition — if you say so upfront and can justify it.
Rules 166 and 157The two rules that block the obvious shortcuts. They are why the pilot dies today.
PilotA small trial in one place before spending real money.

Things you can safely say

“A department can’t buy something new, because the rules make you describe it first. We let them buy the proof first, and the product after.”

“The success criteria are locked before anyone competes, so the goalposts can’t move.”

“A first-time supplier can enter, because we cap the risk instead of asking for turnover.”

“If the trial fails, the state has only paid for finding out — not for a rollout it then has to undo.”

“It needs no change in the law. One state order unlocks the first route, and two of the three routes need nothing at all.”

And when you do not know: say so. “That’s the technical side — let me bring in Lenin.” That is a completely normal answer in a team presentation, and it is far better than guessing. Nobody expects every member to know every part.

The website is real and working — 41 pages, and it runs with the wifi switched off. Four things are designed but not yet built: DigiLocker checks, expert scoring, the sanction paperwork, and the treasury payment link. We say that openly, because it makes everything else we claim believable.
