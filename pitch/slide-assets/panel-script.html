What to say — panel review

GovStart Bridge · SIH26136 · technical walkthrough · about six minutes

Open 20 seconds

Start with the sentence the whole design comes from: a department cannot buy an innovation. Procurement law says describe what you are buying before you buy it, and an innovation is the one thing you cannot describe yet.

So we stopped trying. We split the purchase in two. First you buy the evidence. Then, once that evidence has told you what the thing actually is, you buy the product.

There are four phases, and each one exists to answer a question the one before it leaves open.

Phase 1 — who decides what counts as success? 30 seconds

If you are going to award on evidence, that is the first question — and when do they decide? Because if the yardstick can move after the results are in, nobody trusts the award.

So the officer writes the outcome they need — not a product, an outcome — and the measures that would prove it. We hash that set with SHA-256 and publish it with the challenge, before a single startup has been seen.

Change one number afterwards and the seal visibly breaks. The yardstick is fixed, in public.

Phase 2 — so who is allowed to compete? 30 seconds

Normally a turnover filter and a prior-contract requirement — which excludes exactly the young firm that can solve the problem.

The rule does let you relax that, but only if you declare it upfront and can justify it. Our justification is a risk cap. We score the pilot on five axes: how far damage spreads, what data is exposed, whether it is reversible, what it costs, and who depends on it.

Cap the risk in the contract and turnover stops being a proxy for anything. So we screen on the cap, not the balance sheet. That is the exact condition Rule 173(i) attaches.

Phase 3 — you have picked someone; what are you paying for? 35 seconds

Not a product. That is the whole point.

MSInS signs an Evidence Contract, paid milestone by milestone. The startup works in a sandbox on masked data, in one taluka, under DPDP. Each milestone releases money only when its evidence lands.

And here is the number a finance department cares about: if this fails, the state has spent the cost of finding out — not the cost of a rollout it then has to unwind.

Phase 4 — it worked. Can you actually buy it? 35 seconds

This is the question that kills most pilots.

The validator was named back in phase one, before any result existed. They recompute the seal and check the evidence against the published criteria.

If it passed, the platform picks the route from facts it already holds — a Proprietary Article Certificate, a limited tender to the winners, or the GeM catalogue. That becomes the deployment contract, and the department buys.

And if none of the three applies, it says so. It will not manufacture a route, because a route that does not survive audit is not a route.

Demo — the seal about 1 minute

Have step 1 already open. Seal a challenge, then edit one criterion in front of them.

I am sealing the criteria now … and now I am going to change one target, after the fact.

That is the whole integrity story. I do not need you to trust me — the page tells you.

If there is time, step 2: move the risk slider.

Turnover is a proxy for delivery risk. Cap the risk and the proxy is redundant — which is what 173(i) actually asks for.

Close 20 seconds

So: fix the yardstick, open the gate lawfully, buy the proof cheaply, and have a way out that already exists. Four phases, and no amendment to the General Financial Rules.

Everything I have shown you is running today — you can open it and check. Four things are scoped but not built, and they are on the slide: DigiLocker verification, expert-panel scoring, the sanction builder, and Treasury–PFMS release.

If they ask

Is this really built, or mockups?

Built. Forty-one pages, and it runs offline with no server — I can open it with the wifi off.

What is the tech stack?

Deliberately plain: static HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript. No framework, no backend. A government prototype should demo on a laptop with no network, and a department’s IT should be able to read it end to end.

Where is the data from?

Simulated demonstration data, labelled as such on every page. No server, no accounts, nothing collected.

Is SHA-256 not overkill here?

It is the one place where a person could quietly change a number after results came in. Everything else is process; this is the only part that has to be tamper-evident.

Why not just use GeM?

GeM is one of our three exits. The gap is upstream — GeM cannot help you buy something you cannot specify yet.

Does this need a change in the law?

No. One state Government Resolution unlocks Tier 1, and Tiers 2 and 3 need nothing at all.

What is not built?

Four things: DigiLocker verification, expert-panel scoring, the sanction builder, and Treasury–PFMS release. Scoped, not built.

Who validates, and what stops it being gamed?

The validator is named at step one, before any result exists, and they recompute the seal at step five. That ordering removes both criteria drift and validator shopping.

What happens if the pilot fails?

The state has paid for the evidence and nothing more. There is no product to unwind, no hardware to strip out.

Something you do not know

I have not tested that.  /  That is not built yet.
